President's Brief — three takeaways
  1. The Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern review has become a contested evidentiary fight, and the scheduled deadline to become a party of record is this Friday. On August 26 the applicants filed a response urging the Board to reject opponents' prima facie challenges, arguing the application clears the threshold standard. Under the procedural schedule the Board adopted on August 18, notices of intent to participate are due September 4, with comments and protests due November 18. Whether Amsted Rail files is a commercial and legal judgement rather than an operational one; missing the date would not necessarily foreclose later involvement, which remains subject to the Board's rules and discretion, but it is the clean route onto the service list and it is four days away.
  2. The National Steel Car strike entered its 20th day over the weekend with no settlement reported. More than 1,200 United Steelworkers Local 7135 members in Hamilton have been out since August 12, putting the three-week mark on about September 2. The dispute still turns on wages and on the piecework incentive scheme, which the union links to three deaths at the plant between 2020 and 2022 — a framing that, on the union's account, makes a quick settlement on the company's original terms harder. Plant production status is not established by any source we could read, so treat a shutdown as unconfirmed; the practical exposure remains timing, release schedules and who carries inventory while a build waits.
  3. Grain is the strongest demand signal on the board going into harvest, and it is largely a covered-hopper story. USDA reported year-to-date grain rail carloads at a record for its series, up 16% on last year and 24% above the three-year average, while CPKC closed the 2025–26 Canadian crop year at a record 30.66 million tonnes. Sustained high grain volumes point to heavy covered-hopper activity, which over time is supportive of running-repair and wear-part consumption in wheels, bearings and brake components — less so for side frames and bolsters, which are long-lived structural parts driven more by condemnations, damage and new-car build. The caveat is that loadings measure what moved, not car counts, cycle times or ownership, and much of that equipment is shipper- or lessor-owned, so this is a supportive backdrop rather than a measured demand signal.

Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern ask the STB to reject opponents' prima facie challenges to the merger application
Union Pacific — company news release · Aug 26 · merger proceeding · standing watch item
The applicants filed a response arguing that the revised application "easily satisfies" the Board's threshold standard and that challenges to its sufficiency should be rejected; chief executive Jim Vena said the companies had submitted "an unprecedented amount of evidence." The release repeats the applicants' own projections of more than 88,000 new single-line county-to-county lanes, roughly $1 billion in annual operating savings, about $3.5 billion in annual customer savings from truck-to-rail diversion and 2.1 million truckloads shifted off highways — company figures, not Board findings. Under the procedural schedule adopted August 18, notices of intent to participate are due September 4 and comments, protests and requests for conditions are due November 18. Why it matters: the Board has not ruled on the merits and has not endorsed any of the applicants' numbers, so the status remains a procedural schedule in an active proceeding rather than any form of approval. For a supplier the practical question this week is procedural, not outcome: September 4 is the scheduled deadline to file a notice of intent and become a party of record, which is separate from whether one supports or opposes the transaction, and counsel would be the right judge of whether participation is commercially useful and what routes remain afterwards. Any eventual effect on component purchasing would come from network and shop decisions years out, across the combined railroad, and cannot be read from this filing.
National Steel Car strike reaches day 20; union ties the dispute to the plant's piecework incentive programme
Canadian Occupational Safety, with CBC News (Aug 12) on the strike vote · labour · standing watch item
More than 1,200 United Steelworkers Local 7135 members at the Kenilworth Avenue North plant in Hamilton, Ontario have been on strike since 12:01 a.m. on August 12, after roughly 91% turnout produced about 79% against the company's offer. Local president Frank Crowder frames the dispute as two connected issues — wages that have not kept pace with inflation, and an incentive scheme paying an extra 25% for meeting daily production quotas which he says has been ratcheted up to unsafe levels and contributed to three worker deaths at the plant since 2020. The union says the company left renegotiations; no settlement, resumed bargaining or new offer has been reported, and current plant production status is not established by any source we could read. Why it matters: a dispute framed around fatalities and the pay structure itself is generally harder to settle quickly than a pure wage gap, so a longer stoppage is worth planning for as a scenario, not assuming as the base case — the absence of reported bargaining is not confirmation that none is happening. The near-term supplier effects are about timing and working capital — delivery dates, release schedules and who holds inventory while a build waits — and vary by contract terms and build stage. If the stoppage runs long enough that a buyer seeks replacement capacity, some of that demand may shift to another builder, some may be deferred and some could be lost outright; slots, tooling, approved designs and each builder's own supplier package all limit how freely work moves.
Fourteen cars of a CN train derail at a Truro, Nova Scotia crossing; no injuries and no leaks reported
CBC News, with Global News · Aug 29–30 · safety incident
The derailment happened at the Queen Street crossing in central Nova Scotia at about 5:30 p.m. on Saturday, August 29. Three of the derailed cars carried residue hydrogen peroxide and were secured; the other eleven were carrying mostly paper-related products. No leaks, fires or injuries were reported, officials said there was no danger to public safety, and a detour around the crossing remained in place through Sunday while recovery continued. The cause is under investigation and CN apologised to Truro residents for the disruption. Why it matters: no cause has been determined, so this says nothing yet about track, equipment or component performance and should not be read as either. Its relevance to us is the investigative tail: where Canadian derailment investigations do examine rolling stock, wheels, bearings, trucks and couplers are among the items looked at. Not every derailment draws a formal public investigation, and a component finding might lead only to railroad maintenance action, a safety advisory, or nothing broader. Worth logging as an open file rather than acting on.

CN-served CANXPORT transload hub opens at Prince Rupert, adding up to 400,000 TEUs of rail-to-container capacity
Ray-Mont Logistics, CN and Prince Rupert Port Authority — joint release via PR Newswire · Aug 28 · customer network investment
The C$750 million facility, operated by Montreal-based Ray-Mont Logistics, opened on Friday, August 28 and is the first major project to come online in a planned C$3 billion expansion of the Prince Rupert gateway. It transloads rail-borne petrochemical, forestry, agriculture and mining exports into containers — equipment mix varies by commodity, from covered hoppers and tank cars to boxcars and centrebeam flats — and the partners put its contribution at up to 400,000 twenty-foot equivalent units of annual capacity, with stated headroom beyond that. CN is supporting it by expanding the Zanardi Rapids Bridge and adding rail corridor capacity, and Indigenous partners are involved in the development and operation. Why it matters: the announcement establishes new capacity, not a measured change in how the car fleet works; no cycle-time, car-count or utilisation data was published, so any operational read-through is inference. Directionally, port-side transloading tends to affect how quickly cars are unloaded and returned rather than how far they travel, and it could cut either way for fleet size — incremental export volume would need more cars, while faster turns could move the same volume with fewer. The firmer point is the CN bridge and corridor spending, a customer capital commitment on the Western Canadian corridor that sits alongside the grain capacity picture below.
CPKC closes the 2025–26 Canadian crop year at a record 30.66 million tonnes of grain and grain products
Farms.com, reporting CPKC figures · Aug 5 · customer volumes
CPKC moved 30.66 million tonnes of Canadian grain and grain products in the crop year ended July 31, beating its previous record set in 2020–21 by roughly 72,500 tonnes. The railway reported total grain volume 11% higher than the prior year, 16% above the three-year average and 20% above the five-year average, and attributed the result partly to customer investment in elevators able to load 8,500-foot trains, alongside its own network and asset spending. Why it matters: a record crop year indicates high covered-hopper activity on the CPKC network, which over time is supportive of wear-part and running-repair demand. It does not establish fleet utilisation: tonnage can rise through heavier payloads, longer trains, faster turns or route mix without a proportional increase in car-miles, and CPKC published no car counts, cycle times or ownership split — much of the grain fleet is shipper-, government- or lessor-owned rather than the railway's. Note too that this is CPKC's own Canadian grain measure and is not comparable line-for-line with other railways' grain reporting. Carried today because the new crop year is now under way and the capacity position going into it is the relevant backdrop.

USDA reports record year-to-date grain rail carloads, up 16%, as July fuel surcharges hit a record 61 cents per mile
RFD-TV, reporting USDA Grain Transportation Report data · Aug 4 · demand indicator
Year-to-date grain rail carloads reached a record for USDA's available series, running 16% above last year and 24% above the three-year average, with the agency attributing the pace to elevated corn stocks and export commitments keeping networks busy ahead of harvest. Rail fuel surcharges averaged a record 61 cents per mile on USDA's grain measure in July; the report noted August costs could ease while later diesel increases may lift September charges as harvest shipments accelerate. Why it matters: grain moves overwhelmingly in covered hoppers, so a record carload pace concentrates duty on one part of the fleet and, if sustained through harvest, is supportive of running-repair and wear-part demand in that segment. Where that demand lands depends on ownership — private-car owners, lessors, railroads and contract repair and wheel shops, not automatically any one railroad's purchasing. The surcharge figure is a shipper cost measure, not a railroad margin or a car-supply measure, and high freight costs can pull marginal volume to barge or truck where the geography allows. Treat this as an aftermarket indicator, not evidence about new covered-hopper orders, which turn on expected commodity demand, lease rates, fleet age, scrappage, financing costs and builder slots rather than a single season's loadings.
Port of Los Angeles signs a 30-year extension with Yusen Terminals tied to $200 million in zero-emission equipment
Progressive Railroading · Aug 28 · intermodal
The agreement runs through 2056 and commits Yusen Terminals — an operator at the port since 1991, owned by Ocean Network Express — to $200 million in zero-emission cargo-handling equipment, with electric top handlers, forklifts and yard tractors already in service. On-dock rail at the port is served by Pacific Harbor Line, which interchanges with BNSF and Union Pacific. Why it matters: a thirty-year commitment at the largest US container gateway shows the operator and the port planning around this terminal for the long run; it is evidence of commitment rather than a forecast of cargo growth, since a long concession can justify mandated equipment modernisation without assuring volumes or rail share. The read-through to our categories is indirect and slow: intermodal traffic works double-stack well cars rather than the bulk fleet, and terminal equipment spending is not railcar spending. Useful as background on the San Pedro Bay gateway that feeds the intermodal traffic leading rail growth this year.
