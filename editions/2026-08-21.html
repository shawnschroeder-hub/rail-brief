President's Brief — three takeaways
  1. Supply base — the National Steel Car stoppage is in its tenth day, and the last reliable word on mediation is a week old. Roughly 1,200 USW Local 7135 members have been out since August 12. As of the most recent account our reader could open, dated August 18, an Ontario Ministry of Labour mediator had been assigned but active mediation had not begun; no later report was found in the sources checked, which is not the same as knowing that nothing has since been scheduled. For a component supplier the near-term consequence is that new-build installation demand at that plant is largely suspended; whether inbound shipments are deferred, held as supplier inventory, or eventually cancelled depends on contract terms and how long the stoppage runs.
  2. Regulation — federal attention is concentrating on who is qualified to be in the cab, not on what the equipment must be. The FRA has proposed making English-language proficiency a condition of certifying and recertifying locomotive engineers and conductors, and would codify a 10-route-mile limit on operations by Mexican crews at the southern border. Separately the STB adopted targeted updates to its own procedural regulations on August 18. Neither touches car or component specifications, so there is no direct read-through to undercarriage demand; the exposure sits with customers' crew availability and cross-border operating practice, particularly on Mexican interchange, which could affect train velocity and equipment cycles indirectly.
  3. Demand — the import forecast points down for August before flattening, which argues for caution about extrapolating this summer's intermodal strength. The National Retail Federation and Hackett Associates project August container imports at 2.22 million TEUs, down 4.2% year over year, with the following four months in a narrow +0.3% to +2.8% band and the full year essentially flat. That is a forecast rather than an outcome, and port import TEUs are not the same measure as railroad intermodal loads — transloading, inland routing and the share of imports that moves by rail all sit in between. Still, it is a reminder that the intermodal contribution to traffic growth may be nearer a plateau than a trend.

FRA proposes English-language proficiency as a certification requirement for engineers and conductors
Washington Times account of the FRA proposal · Aug 13 · proposed rule
The proposal would require locomotive engineers and conductors to demonstrate enough English to communicate with US safety inspectors and read operational bulletins — proficiency sufficient to meet safety standards rather than full fluency — and would codify a 10-route-mile geographic limit on operations by Mexican crews at the southern border. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said that on "a massive freight train, you need to be proficient in our national language." This is a notice of proposed rulemaking, published in the Federal Register in late July, with comments running through late September; it is not a final rule and nothing takes effect yet. Why it matters: the requirement lands on crew qualification, not on equipment, so there is no read-through to car or component specifications. The item worth tracking is the border provision — if adopted as proposed it would constrain how far Mexican crews may run into the US, which bears on cross-border interchange practice rather than on what is bolted under the car.
National Steel Car strike is in its tenth day; no active mediation as of the latest available report
CHCH News, Hamilton · Aug 18 · labour · status carried forward
More than 1,200 United Steelworkers Local 7135 members have been out since 12:01 a.m. on August 12, after a vote with about 91% turnout rejected the company's offer by roughly 80%. The union says the company walked out of renegotiations the week before; an Ontario Ministry of Labour mediator has been assigned and is described as available, but active mediation had not begun as of August 18, the date of the most recent account our reader could open; no later report was found in the sources checked. The dispute turns on wages against inflation and on a piecework incentive paying an extra 25% of base wage once a daily production quota is met, which the union links to three deaths at the Kenilworth Avenue North plant since 2020. Why it matters: this is a car builder that installs undercarriage components, so new-build installation demand at that plant is largely suspended while the line is stopped. Railcar work does not move quickly between builders, so the realistic near-term effect is deferred delivery schedules and possible inventory build at suppliers rather than volume reallocated to other plants; only a prolonged stoppage would be likely to prompt customers to seek replacement capacity or affect future awards.
Port Tracker: container imports forecast to fall 4.2% in August, then flatten through year end
Progressive Railroading, reporting NRF and Hackett Associates data · Aug 10 · demand forecast
The National Retail Federation and Hackett Associates put August at 2.22 million TEUs, down 4.2% year over year, followed by September at 2.16 million (+2.8%), October 2.13 million (+2.7%), November 2.03 million (+0.3%) and December 2.06 million (+2.5%). Full-year 2026 is projected at 25.5 million TEUs against 25.4 million in 2025, which NRF and Hackett characterise as an increase of 0.1% — the rounded totals shown here cannot reproduce that percentage precisely, so it rests on their unrounded figures. Why it matters: these are forecasts from a retail trade body and a consultancy, not booked volumes, and port import TEUs are not interchangeable with railroad intermodal loads. Read against intermodal's strong year to date, the projection suggests the import-driven component of that growth may be closer to flat than to accelerating. That does bear on wear parts: well and spine cars in high-utilisation intermodal service are significant consumers of wheels, brake components and truck wear parts, so a plateau in intermodal mileage would in time show up in replacement demand, separately from the new-build and general carload streams.

UP–NS merger: the August 18 procedural schedule stands, with no further Board action this week
Surface Transportation Board — latest news · Aug 18 · Docket FD 36873 · status carried forward
The Board's most recent action in its review of Union Pacific's proposed acquisition of Norfolk Southern remains the August 18 decision adopting a procedural schedule for consideration of the revised major merger application; comments are due November 18 and the schedule carries the last steps into 2027. No later Board action was found on the agency's news page — filings can be served into the docket without generating a release, so this is the limit of what our reader could check. There is likewise no public update from the Belt Railway of Chicago's weekly Clearing Yard reporting, required after the Board's August 3 letter citing a 57% year-over-year rise in average weekly railcar inventory and dwell of roughly 31 hours against about 18 a year earlier. Why it matters: the schedule is the Board's own and the applicants' 2027 completion target is theirs, not a Board finding. The useful planning point is that no decision is imminent, so only investment explicitly contingent on the transaction should be assumed to be waiting on it. Clearing Yard inventory and dwell are the nearer-term indicator of Chicago gateway fluidity: sustained dwell reduces car turns and effective fleet capacity, which cuts both ways for a supplier — it depresses velocity-linked mileage while tending to raise the number of cars needed to move the same freight.

STB adopts targeted updates to its own regulations to streamline proceedings
Surface Transportation Board · Aug 18 · agency news release
The Board says it "adopted targeted updates to its regulations to improve efficiency and align procedures with current practice," principally through revised delegations of authority. This is an internal procedural change to how the agency handles its own docket, not a change to any substantive rail regulation; the release our reader could open does not state an effective date. Why it matters: the effect is administrative — potentially faster handling of routine matters before the Board — and it carries no equipment, safety or rate implications. It is worth noting only because it was adopted the same day as the merger schedule and should not be mistaken for a substantive action in that proceeding.

Greenbrier's European arm takes an order for 70 Eanos wagons from two German operators
Railway Supply weekly review · week of Jul 26–Aug 1 · Europe · car builder
STORK Cargo and Mindener Kreisbahnen jointly ordered 70 Eanos open wagons from Greenbrier Leasing Europe, each a four-axle vehicle offering about 85 cubic metres of loading volume and a maximum payload of 63.5 tonnes, fitted with telematics for fleet monitoring; delivery is expected in autumn 2026. No contract value was disclosed, and the report does not make clear whether the wagons are being sold outright or supplied on lease, so this should not be read as confirmed manufacturing backlog. Why it matters: the volume is small and European wagon platforms use different running gear from North American three-piece trucks, so there is no direct read-across to North American component demand. It is carried as a marker of a watched competitor's activity outside North America, and because telematics was specified on this order — worth watching as a trend, though one contract does not establish it as standard across new-build European wagons.
